This was such a fun and exciting collaboration! 🧠🔊 I worked closely with first authors Jason Hou and Md Osman Goni Nayeem, and with Sarah Ornellas from [Conformable Decoders, led by Prof. Canan Dagdeviren](https://conformabledecoders.media.mit.edu/), at the [MIT Media Lab](https://www.media.mit.edu/). My part of the project focused on the acoustic characterization and in vitro biocompatibility of ImPULS.

## What is ImPULS?

ImPULS is an implantable piezoelectric ultrasound stimulator designed to modulate neuronal activity in deep brain regions. The flexible device is approximately the thickness of a human hair and uses a biocompatible piezoelectric material to generate localized ultrasound fields.

At resonance, ImPULS can produce focal pressures of up to 100 kPa while maintaining a very small cross-sectional footprint. Unlike conventional electrodes used for deep-brain stimulation, it has no exposed electrochemically active surface. This avoids some of the corrosion and biofouling concerns associated with chronic electrical interfaces.

## My contribution

The acoustic characterization helped establish how the device's geometry and resonant behavior translated into pressure output under tissue-relevant conditions. This is an essential step for an implantable ultrasound device: the stimulation target depends not only on where the implant is placed, but also on the frequency, pressure, and spatial extent of the generated field.

The in vitro experiments addressed a complementary question: whether the encapsulated device and its operation were compatible with nearby cells. Together, these measurements connected the engineering performance of ImPULS with the biological environment in which it is intended to operate.

## Why it matters

Implanting the source of ultrasound close to the target can avoid the scattering and loss of spatial precision that occur when ultrasound must propagate through the skull. In the study, ImPULS was used to modulate neuronal activity in deep subcortical regions and influence dopamine production along long-range neural projections in mice.

The device is still an experimental research platform, but its small, flexible form factor offers an intriguing alternative for localized deep-brain stimulation and a potentially useful tool for studying neural circuits.
